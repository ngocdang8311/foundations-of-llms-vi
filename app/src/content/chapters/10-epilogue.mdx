## 10. Epilogue: how transformers inherit the story

At the end of section 9, attention was helping two RNNs: the encoder still read the source one word after the other, and the decoder still wrote the translation one word after the other. But in June 2017, **a team at Google removed the RNNs, and kept only attention**. Their architecture, the *transformer*, is the basis of all the large language models of today.

Although we will not study it in detail here, I want to highlight something: before 2019 (and GPT-2), transformers were not meant to be used to create LLMs and chatbots as we know them today, but just as a means to solve the classical NLP tasks of classification and translation! Let's see how this story started.

### 10.1 Transformers instead of RNNs

In RNNs, recurrence has two costs. The first is *gradient flow*. As we saw in exercise 7.2a, between two distant words the information and the gradient go through every step in between, and even in an LSTM, the gradient that reaches a word shrinks with its distance to the end of the text. The second cost is *the time it takes*. To compute the state after word $t$, an RNN needs the state after word $t - 1$, so the L states of a text are computed one after the other.

<PaperNote id="vaswani2017" look="Figures 3 to 5, in the appendix of the arXiv version: the weights of a few heads of the fifth layer of the encoder, drawn as lines between the words of a sentence.">

The paper was posted on arXiv on 12 June 2017 by eight authors, most of them at Google Brain and Google Research. The base model has 65 million parameters, with vectors of 512 and 8 heads, and the big model 213 million, with vectors of 1,024 and 16 heads.  Its encoder is a stack of 6 layers of self-attention, and its decoder a stack of 6 layers that attend to the translation written so far, and to the vectors of the encoder. On the translation of English into German of WMT'14, their big model reached a BLEU score of 28.4, more than 2 points above all the previous models, ensembles included, and 41.8 on English into French. The base model was trained in 12 hours, on a single machine with 8 GPUs, and the big model in 3.5 days.
</PaperNote>

A GPU is fast because it does thousands of operations at the same time, but within a given piece of text, an RNN cannot give it many operations to do in parallel. In the words of Vaswani et al., *"This inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples, which becomes critical at longer sequence lengths"* [@vaswani2017].

The idea of the transformer is to replace the recurrence by attention inside the text itself. In section 9.2, the decoder compared its state with the states of all the words of the source. In *self-attention*, every word of a text does the same with all the words of the same text, itself included.

Each word gives a score to every word of the text, a dot product as in section 9.2 (computed after learned linear transformations of the vectors, which we skip here). A softmax turns the scores into weights, and the new vector of the word is the weighted average of the vectors of all the words. This removes the two costs of recurrence. Any two words are linked in a single step, whatever the distance between them, so the information and the gradient no longer go through all the words in between. And the new vectors of all the words are computed at the same time, with a few products of matrices, which is what a GPU does best. A transformer stacks several such layers: each one computes new vectors from the vectors of the layer below.

A weighted average does not depend on the order of the words, so self-attention alone would read a text as a bag of words, like the averages of section 4.3. This is why the input vector of each word is its embedding plus a vector that encodes its position in the text.

Indeed we tend to forget it today, as "transformer model" is almost synonymous with "autoregressive LLM", but the task for which it was invented was translation!

Its encoder, on the left of the figure, is a stack of layers of self-attention that read the source sentence. Its decoder, on the right, attends to the vectors of the encoder, as the decoder of section 9.2 did, and it also uses self-attention on the translation written so far. During the training, the decoder computes its predictions for all the positions of the translation at once, so its self-attention needs a *mask* (the "Masked Multi-Head Attention" of the figure): each position may attend only to itself and to the positions before it, and not to the words that it must predict. In the words of the paper, the mask is there "to prevent positions from attending to subsequent positions".

### 10.2 BERT

The transformer was made for translation, but if we focus only on its encoder part, it does exactly what we were looking for in section 8: it is a network that reads a whole text and gives each word a vector that depends on all the other words (and that trains much faster than an LSTM). In October 2018, Jacob Devlin and his colleagues at Google pre-trained a transformer encoder on raw text, and fine-tuned it for each task, as ULMFiT did with an LSTM [@devlin2018]. They called it *BERT*, for *bidirectional encoder representations from transformers*. Once again, they were not trying to create an autoregressive LLM able to generate text; they were simply looking for a better encoder to then perform the usual downstream NLP tasks.

But how do we pre-train the encoder? In other words, which task can train an encoder on raw text? A language model predicts the next word from the words before it, so it ignores the words after. This is a waste for an encoder, whose job is to read the whole text: in their words, *"The major limitation is that standard language models are unidirectional"*. ELMo got around it with two language models, one for each direction (section 8.2), but each of them reads only one side of the word. And a transformer encoder cannot be trained as a language model at all: every word attends to every word, so the word to predict would be in its input.

**BERT hides words instead.** In each text, 15% of the tokens are chosen at random and replaced by a special token, [MASK], and BERT must predict them from all the other tokens, on both sides. (To avoid a gap with the fine-tuning, where nothing is masked, a chosen token is replaced by [MASK] 80% of the time, by a random token 10% of the time, and left as it is 10% of the time.) This *masked language model* was "inspired by the Cloze task", a test of readability from 1953, where readers fill in the missing words of a text. It is also the task of CBOW in section 5.2, predicting a word from the words around it, except that the context is now the whole text, and the model a deep transformer instead of an average of embeddings.

BERT was pre-trained on 3.3 billion words: BooksCorpus, 800 million words of unpublished books, and the English Wikipedia, 2,500 million words. Its vocabulary has 30,000 *WordPieces*, subwords like those of the box of section 7.3, so no word is unknown. It comes in two sizes. BERT base has 12 layers, vectors of 768 and 12 heads, 110 million parameters. BERT large has 24 layers, vectors of 1,024 and 16 heads, 340 million parameters. Each pre-training took 4 days, on Google's own processors, the TPUs.

Now with a simple encoder, we associate a latent vector to *every word* in the text. If we want to do a classification task, this is not what we need, we need a fixed length representation for the *whole* text. **How does BERT turn a text of any length into a vector of fixed size?** With a trick that brings back the box of section 3: **a special token, [CLS]** (for *classification*), is added at the start of every text, and BERT computes a new vector for it, as for every other token:

$$
x_{\text{CLS}}\, x_1 \dots x_L \longrightarrow h_{\text{CLS}}\, h_1 \dots h_L.
$$

[CLS] is not a word of the text, so its vector has nothing of its own to represent: through the attention of its 12 layers, it collects what it needs from all the words. A second pre-training task trains it: BERT reads two sentences, and must tell from the vector of [CLS] whether the second one follows the first one in the text, or was drawn at random. In the words of the paper, "The final hidden state corresponding to this token is used as the aggregate sequence representation for classification tasks." **The vector of [CLS] is the fixed-size representation, and the head of a classifier reads it.**

To fine-tune BERT for a classification task, add a head that reads $h_{\text{CLS}}$, and train the whole network on the labeled examples, for 2 to 4 epochs, with a small learning rate: the authors recommend values between 2e-5 and 5e-5. This is the recipe of ULMFiT, without its gradual unfreezing. BERT set a new state of the art on eleven tasks. On GLUE, a benchmark of nine tasks of sentence understanding, the score went from 72.8, for GPT (section 10.3), to 80.5 for BERT large. BERT can also be used as ELMo was: frozen, with its vectors given as features to another network. On a task of named entity recognition, the concatenation of the vectors of its last four layers was "only 0.3 F1 behind fine-tuning the entire model".

<PaperNote id="devlin2018" look="Figure 1: pre-training and fine-tuning use the same network, and only the output layers change. “During fine-tuning, all parameters are fine-tuned.”">

BERT was pre-trained on batches of 256 sequences of up to 512 tokens, for 1,000,000 steps, "approximately 40 epochs over the 3.3 billion word corpus". To save time, the sequences had 128 tokens for 90% of the steps. The base model took 4 days on 16 TPU chips, and the large one 4 days on 64. The paper measures what the two pre-training tasks bring (Table 5). Without the task on pairs of sentences, BERT base loses a little on question answering and on inference between sentences. Without this task, and trained as a language model from left to right instead, like GPT, it loses on every task, for instance from 86.5 to 77.5 on MRPC, a task of paraphrases, and from 87.9 to 77.8 F1 on SQuAD, a task of question answering. The masked language model, which reads both sides of every word, is what makes the difference.

</PaperNote>

The other vectors, $h_1 \dots h_L$, are contextual embeddings, like those of ELMo in section 8.2, except that each of them depends on all the words of the text, on both sides, at every layer. Let's look at "bank" again.

### 10.3 GPT-1 to GPT-3, then InstructGPT

A few months before BERT, in June 2018, Alec Radford and his colleagues at OpenAI had taken the other half of the transformer, the decoder [@radford2018]. A decoder is a language model: thanks to the mask of section 10.1, each token attends only to the tokens before it, so it can be trained on raw text to predict the next token, like the LSTM of section 7.3, but with all the positions of a text computed at once. Their model, now called GPT-1 (GPT for *generative pre-training*), has 12 layers, vectors of 768 and 12 heads, and reads up to 512 tokens. It was trained on BooksCorpus, more than 7,000 unpublished books, chosen because "it contains long stretches of contiguous text, which allows the generative model to learn to condition on long-range information".

Then GPT-1 is fine-tuned for each task, with a head, as ULMFiT and BERT. But in a decoder, the vector of a token depends only on the tokens before it, so the first token cannot summarize the text, as [CLS] does in BERT. The only token that has read the whole text is the last one. So GPT-1 adds a special token at the end of the text, the *extract* token, and the head reads its vector:

$$
x_1 \dots x_L \, x_{\text{ext}} \longrightarrow h_1 \dots h_L \, h_{\text{ext}}.
$$

Fine-tuned in this way, GPT-1 improved the state of the art on 9 of the 12 tasks of the paper. The authors also noticed something else: without any fine-tuning, the language model alone could do some tasks. To find the sentiment of a sentence, they add the word "very" at its end, and compare the probabilities that the model gives to the next words "positive" and "negative". These tricks work better and better as the pre-training goes on (their Figure 2).

GPT-2, in February 2019, pushed this idea to its end [@radford2019]. The architecture is the same, only larger: up to 48 layers and 1.5 billion parameters, reading 1,024 tokens. It was trained on WebText, 40 GB of text from 8 million web pages: the pages linked from Reddit by posts with at least 3 karma, a sign that some readers found them interesting. And GPT-2 is not fine-tuned at all. The claim of the paper is in its abstract: "language models begin to learn these tasks without any explicit supervision". A task can be written as text, for instance a translation as "(translate to french, english text, french text)", and a language model trained on enough text has met such sequences, and learns to continue them, because this predicts their next words. To summarize an article, GPT-2 reads it followed by "TL;DR:", the abbreviation that people write on the web before a summary. To translate, it reads a few pairs written as "english sentence = french sentence", then an English sentence followed by "=". These results were modest, for instance 11.5 BLEU from French to English. But on language modeling, they were spectacular.

Without a single update of its weights on WikiText-2, GPT-2 beat every model trained on it: its smallest version, which has the size of GPT-1, has a test perplexity of 29.41, against 39.14 for the previous best, and the largest one 18.34.

<PaperNote id="radford2019" look="Table 3: the results of the four models, without any training, on the datasets of language modeling of the time, WikiText-2 among them.">

The four models of the paper have 12, 24, 36 and 48 layers, with vectors of 768 to 1,600; the smallest one has the size of GPT-1. Wikipedia was removed from WebText, "since it is a common data source for other datasets and could complicate analysis due to overlapping training data with test evaluation tasks". The authors still measured the overlap: 1.63% of the 8-grams of the test set of WikiText-2 appear in WebText, while 0.66% of them already appear in the training set of WikiText-2. Their idea of the task written as text goes far: "a language model with sufficient capacity will begin to learn to infer and perform the tasks demonstrated in natural language sequences in order to better predict them". For summaries, the hint "TL;DR:" is worth 6.4 points of the ROUGE score, a score that counts the n-grams in common with summaries written by people. GPT-2 translates French into English with 11.5 BLEU, although the authors had removed the pages that are not in English from WebText, and found only 10 MB of French left in it.

</PaperNote>

You probably know the rest of the story. GPT-3, in May 2020, is GPT-2 made a hundred times larger [@brown2020]: 175 billion parameters, 96 layers, vectors of 12,288, a context of 2,048 tokens, trained on 300 billion tokens from the web, books and Wikipedia. Its paper gave the idea of GPT-2 a name, *in-context learning*: the task is described in the text given to the model, the *prompt*, with a few examples, and the model continues the text.

This is the end point of our second question. In sections 2 to 4, nothing was transferred from one task to another. Section 5 transferred the embeddings, section 8 the whole encoder, and BERT and GPT-1 transfer everything but the head. With GPT-3, even the head is gone. The answer to a task is the next words of the text, so the output layer of the language model, which section 8 dropped, is the head of every task, and nothing is trained for the task anymore. **The task is written in the prompt, and the pre-trained language model does all the work.**

One step was still missing. A language model continues texts as they are written on the web, which is not the same as doing what the person who writes the prompt asks for. As the authors of InstructGPT put it, the task of "predicting the next token on a webpage from the internet [...] is different from the objective “follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely”" [@ouyang2022]. In March 2022, they trained GPT-3 to follow instructions, in three steps: fine-tuning on answers written by people, training a reward model on the preferences of people between several answers, and optimizing the language model against this reward model with reinforcement learning. The result was striking: "outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters". This stage, called *post-training*, turns a language model into an assistant. It is the topic of another part of the course.
