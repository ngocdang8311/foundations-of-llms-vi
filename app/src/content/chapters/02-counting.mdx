## 2. Counting words: n-grams as the simplest language models

Since language models will be our main interest later in the series, it is worth starting with a historical example representing the simplest way of generating language in an autoregressive way: **the Markov model**.

<PaperNote id="markov1913">
Markov had defined his (now famous) "chains" in 1906, but this paper from 1913 is their first application to a text: the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's book "Eugene Onegin". In this paper, Markov computed the transition probabilities between vowels and consonants in the text.
</PaperNote>

### 2.1 Markov model

If you really want the simplest (and most inefficient) language model, you can simply draw words at random in the vocabulary, following their natural frequency in the language. This is called the *unigram* model, for which
$$
p(x_{i} \mid x_1 \dots x_{i-1}) = p(x_i) = \frac{C(x_i)}{N}
$$
where $C(x_i)$ is the count of word $x_i$ in the training corpus, and $N = \sum C(x_i)$ is the corpus size. This is a very crude model! And we can at least improve it by considering *bigrams*, i.e. the Markov chain model.

#### Simple Markov chain

In the simplest *bigram* Markov chain model, **the probability of a word in a sequence only depends on the word that comes just before it**.
$$
p(x_{i} \mid x_1 \dots x_{i-1}) = p(x_i \mid x_{i-1})
$$
This assumption is of course incorrect for actual human languages, but it allows for very simple modeling using only a transition matrix.

Using a training set, we simply count the occurrences of every word $C(v)$ and of every pair of consecutive words $C(v,w)$. We then use it to compute the probability $p(w \mid v) = C(v,w)/C(v)$ that the word $w$ follows the word $v$. These probabilities define a transition matrix that can then be used for autoregressive text generation using a Markov Chain model.

#### n-gram models

We can also extend the model by using *n-grams*, that is, chunks of $n$ consecutive words. To predict the next word, we thus consider the sequence of the $n-1$ previous words as an input. The Markov chain model of 2.1 is $n = 2$, the unigram model is $n=1$ where no context at all is used.

Using n-grams, the transition matrix will be $V^{n-1} \times V$, which can quickly become overwhelming (for $n=3$ and $V=30{,}000$, we have about $3 \times 10^{13}$ entries). In practice, once again **we store only the n-grams that occur, in a dictionary of counts**, but not the full table.

<PaperNote id="shannon1948" look="Section 3, “The Series of Approximations to English”.">

To measure the information produced by English text, the famous mathematician **Claude Shannon** models it as a random process that writes one symbol after the other: letters drawn independently, then depending on the previous letter, then on the two previous ones, and the same with words. He calls these "Markov processes". And he made his samples by hand! Open a book at random, pick a letter, then open another page, read until you meet this letter, and write down the one that follows it. No wonder he found that *"the labor involved becomes enormous at the next stage"*!
</PaperNote>

### 2.2 Comparing probability distributions

In language modeling, our goal is to approximate the "true" probability distribution of next word occurrence. Let's call it $p(x_{L+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_L)$. Any candidate language model will produce a candidate distribution $q(x_{L+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_L)$ (in the n-gram Markov model, it depends only on $x_{L-n+2} \dots x_L$).

How do we measure success? That is, how do we quantify how similar $q$ is to $p$?

#### KL Divergence and cross-entropy loss

To assess whether a certain probability distribution is a good approximation to another one, the tool of choice is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**
$$
D_{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
$$
I write it with generic distribution $p$ and $q$, but note that this definition also works using conditional probabilities for instance in our case $p(x_{L+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_L)$ and $q(x_{L+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_L)$.

Finding the distribution $q$ that best approximates a given $p$ means **minimizing the KL divergence**. Now, in our case, as $p$ a priori is unknown, exactly computing this quantity might seem impossible. But to minimize the KL divergence, we can first notice that its log part can be separated into

$$
D_{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log p(x) - \sum_x p(x) \log q(x)
$$

The first part is (minus) **the entropy of $p$** (which doesn't depend on $q$), while the second part is the so-called **cross-entropy**.

Since the cross-entropy
$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x).
$$
is the only part that actually depends on $q$, we are actually seeking $q$ that minimizes it.

**Finding $q$ that minimizes the KL-divergence $D_{KL}(p \,\|\, q)$ is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy $H(p,q)$.**

#### Likelihood

Now let's return to our notations, and remember that we are working with conditional probabilities $p(x \mid c)$ and $q(x \mid c)$ of having $x$ as the next word, knowing the context $c=(x_1,\dots,x_L)$.

If $q$ depends on some parameters $\theta$, we seek to minimize the cross-entropy
$$
-\sum_x p(x \mid c) \log q_{\theta}(x \mid c),
$$
which can be seen as an expectation over distribution probability $x \sim p( . | c)$
$$
-\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p( \cdot \mid c)}[\log q_{\theta}(x \mid c)].
$$

Once again, *we cannot compute this quantity directly* because we do not know $p$, but **our text corpus is a sample from $p$**.

At each position $i$ of a text, the context $x_{<i} = x_1 \dots x_{i-1}$ is followed by the word $x_i$ that was actually written. Most contexts appear only once, so we never see the distribution $p(\cdot \mid x_{<i})$, only one word drawn from it. But the average over the $N$ positions of the text estimates the cross-entropy, averaged over the contexts as they occur in the language:

$$
\hat{H} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log q_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})
$$

This average has a simple meaning. A sum of logs is the log of a product, and by the chain rule of section 1.2, $\prod_i q_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ is the probability that the model gives to the whole text, its *likelihood*. So $\hat{H}$ is the negative log-likelihood of the text divided by its number of words, and **minimizing the cross-entropy amounts to maximizing the likelihood of the corpus**.

To train a model, we choose the parameters $\theta$ (for instance the weights of a neural network) that minimize $\hat{H}$ on a *training* text. Each term of the sum is itself a cross-entropy: the one between $q_\theta$ and the distribution that puts all its probability on the observed word (a *one-hot* distribution), which stands in for the unknown $p$:

$$
L_{CE} = -\sum_x \mathbf{1}[x = x_i] \log q_\theta(x \mid x_{<i}) = -\log q_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})
$$

This is the *cross-entropy loss* used to train language models, LLMs included. Such a single term is a noisy estimate, but on average over the words of the text, its gradient is $\nabla_\theta H(p, q_\theta)$. Since the entropy of $p$ does not depend on $\theta$, this is also $\nabla_\theta D_{KL}(p \,\|\, q_\theta)$.

**Maximizing the likelihood = Minimizing the cross-entropy loss = Minimizing the KL divergence**.

For the n-gram models, we don't even need gradients: the ratio of counts $C(v,w)/C(v)$ of section 2.1 is exactly the $q$ that maximizes the likelihood of the training text.

#### Perplexity

Now a cross-entropy is not easy to read and interpret, so we usually report its exponential, the *perplexity* of the model on a text:

$$
{\cal P} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log q(x_i \mid x_{<i})\right) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{q(x_i \mid x_{<i})}\right)^{1/N}
$$

It is the inverse of the geometric average of the probabilities that the model gives to the words of the text. A model that is uniform over $V$ words has perplexity $V$, so **perplexity can be interpreted the number of words the model hesitates between.**

<PaperNote id="shannon1951" look="Section 6: the entropy of English estimated from the guesses, with up to 100 letters of context.">

Three years later, Shannon decided to use people as a language model! He shows them a text, asks them to guess the next letter again and again, and estimates the entropy of English from the number of guesses they need: between 0.6 and 1.3 bits per letter with 100 letters of context. Compare with 4.14 bits from the letter frequencies alone, and 3.3 bits from triples of letters. One bit per letter means a perplexity of 2: with enough context, people hesitate between about two letters!

</PaperNote>

<Optional title="Validation and test sets" subtitle="why we need two held-out texts">

Now, *on which text should we measure it?* Not on the training text: the model was fitted to these very words, so its perplexity there is too optimistic. A model that memorized the training text would give it a very high probability, and still predict new text badly. To estimate how well $q$ approximates $p$, we need another sample from $p$ that the model has never seen: a *held-out* set.

WikiText-2 comes with two held-out sets. The *validation set* is used for every choice we make about the model, such as the value of n (or the weights of the interpolation we'll introduce in section 2.3): we try several values and keep the one with the lowest validation perplexity. But a choice made this way is fitted to the validation set a little, so the validation perplexity of the chosen model is slightly optimistic too. The *test set* is used only once, at the end, to measure the perplexity we report.

</Optional>

### 2.3 Curse of dimensionality

Zero counts arrive fast with words taken from a training corpus. With a vocabulary of 33,000 words, there are about 1.1 billion possible word pairs, but 2 million training words show at most 2 million of them (in exercise 2.1 we had about 618,000 distinct pairs, so 0.06%). Unseen pairs already appear in held-out text at n = 2 or 3. This is a manifestation of **the curse of dimensionality**: the number of possible contexts grows exponentially as $V^{n-1}$ while the training text stays the same size.

There are several options to avoid the infinite perplexity due to zero probabilities associated with the curse of dimensionality:
    - **Smoothing:** giving unseen sequences a small non-zero probability. The simplest is *add-one smoothing* (add 1 to every count), but it works badly with words: it gives too much probability to the billions of unseen pairs.
    - **Interpolation** works better: it mixes the n-gram estimate with the (n−1)-gram estimate, using a weight tuned on validation data. The estimate is applied step-by-step (recursively) down to the unigram, and since the unigram is non-zero for every word in the vocabulary, the mix is never zero.
    - More sophisticated techniques have been devised since, see for instance the Kneser-Ney below, that has long been state-of-the-art.

<Optional title="Backoff and Kneser-Ney" subtitle="the best smoothing of the counting era">

Many variants of smoothing followed, such as *backoff* [@katz1987], which falls back on the (n−1)-gram estimate only when the n-gram was never seen. The best of them, according to the large comparison of Chen and Goodman [@chen1999], is *Kneser-Ney smoothing*. Its trick is in the shorter contexts: a word gets a probability according to the number of different words it follows, rather than according to its count (think of "Francisco", frequent but almost always after "San"). It remained for years the baseline that neural language models had to beat.

</Optional>

Smoothing deals with unseen sequences of known words. Unseen *words* are another problem, which WikiText-2 hides by replacing rare words with `<unk>`. This is our first encounter with the vocabulary problem: it comes back in section 3.2, but *tokenizers* (the topic of the next post) solve it.
