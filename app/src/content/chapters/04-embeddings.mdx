## 4. Dense word embeddings

At the end of section 3, we saw **the first limit of the bag of words: each word has its own dimension**, so for the model, "superb" is as different from "excellent" as it is from "boring". Let's fix this.

### 4.1 The embedding matrix

The idea is to represent each word by a *dense* vector of real numbers, in a space whose dimension D is much smaller than the size V of the vocabulary: a few hundred dimensions (for instance $D = 300$) for tens of thousands of words. This vector is called the *embedding* of the word.

<PaperNote id="hinton1986" look="Figure 6: the weights from the 24 people to the 6 units. Unit 1 separates the English from the Italians, unit 2 encodes the generation, and unit 6 the branch of the family.">

Hinton calls such vectors *distributed representations*: a concept is spread over many units, instead of having its own unit, as in a one-hot vector. Here he trains a small neural network on two family trees with the same shape, one English and one Italian, with 24 people in total and 12 relationships (father, aunt, nephew...). Given a person and a relationship, the network must output the other person. Each person enters as a one-hot vector, and goes through a layer of only 6 units: the ancestor of our embeddings. Nobody told the network what to put there, yet one unit learned the nationality, one the generation, and one the branch of the family. Trained on 100 of the 104 facts, it got the 4 others right in one run, and 3 of them in another.

</PaperNote>

The embeddings of all the words are stored in an *embedding matrix* $W$, with one column per word of the vocabulary:

$$
W \in {\cal M}_{D \times V}(\mathbb{R})
$$

Looking up the embedding of a word is the same as multiplying $W$ by the one-hot vector of this word: the product selects the column of the word.

The benefit of dense embeddings over the sparse vectors of the bag of words is that we can compare them, and measure how similar two words are. The usual measure is the **cosine similarity**, the cosine of the angle between the two vectors, which does not depend on their lengths:

$$
\cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \, \|v\|}
$$

It is 1 when the two vectors point in the same direction, 0 when they are orthogonal, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. Note that the one-hot vectors of two different words are always orthogonal: *in the bag of words, the similarity between two different words is always 0*.

Let's assume for now that we have a good embedding matrix (we will see how to get one, and as you might guess, we will learn it). We can hope that similar words get similar vectors: a high similarity between "excellent" and "superb", but a low one between "ball" and "crocodile". This is what makes a good embedding: **the similarity between the vectors reflects the similarity between the meanings of the words**. A model that has learned something about "excellent" then knows something about "superb" too.

<Optional title="Latent Semantic Analysis" subtitle="another way to get dense vectors">

There are other ways to get dense vectors than learning them with a neural network. For example, take the matrix of the counts of section 3, with one row per word and one column per document, and compress it with a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD): each word gets a dense vector, and the words that appear in the same documents get similar vectors. This is *Latent Semantic Analysis*.

</Optional>

<PaperNote id="deerwester1990" look="Figure 1: nine titles of technical memos and twelve words, in two dimensions. The query “human computer interaction” ends up close to two titles that share no word with it.">

The problem of Deerwester and his colleagues is the one of Salton and Sparck Jones (section 3): find the documents that match a query. But as they put it, "there are usually many ways to express a given concept, so the literal terms in a user's query may not match those of a relevant document". Their solution: decompose the matrix of the word counts of the documents with an SVD, and keep about 100 dimensions. Words and documents become dense vectors, and a query can find a document without sharing a single word with it. The gain was clear on a collection of medical abstracts (13% better precision than plain word matching), but there was none on another collection.

</PaperNote>

Now, **simply using word embeddings does not solve our problem of variable input size.** We replace the L words of a text with L vectors of size D: this is still not a representation of fixed size! So we need a way to *aggregate* these L vectors into a single vector, whose size does not depend on L. Here are three simple options:
- **the average** of the L vectors, of size D;
- **a weighted average**, to give more importance to some words, also of size D;
- **the concatenation** of the vectors of the last n − 1 words only, of size (n − 1) × D, as the n-gram models of section 2 did with the words themselves.

The concatenation keeps the order of the last words, but forgets everything before them. This suits language modeling, where the next word depends mostly on the last ones, so this is where we start.

### 4.2 Concatenation → Bengio's neural language model

Let's go back to language modeling for a moment. With the n-gram models of section 2, we assumed that the probability of the next word depends only on the n − 1 previous words. We can keep this assumption, but replace the table of counts with **a neural network, whose input is the concatenation of the embeddings** of the n − 1 previous words: a vector of fixed size (n − 1) × D.

This is the idea of the influential paper of *Bengio et al.* from 2003, which can be considered as **the first neural language model**. Its architecture is simple: look up the embeddings $e_{L-n+2}, \dots, e_L$ of the last n − 1 words in the matrix $W$, concatenate them into a vector $c$, then feed $c$ into a feedforward network with one hidden layer:

<PaperNote id="bengio2003" look="Figure 1, the architecture: the table C (our W), shared by all the positions, then the tanh layer and the softmax, with the mention “most computation here”. And Table 1 for the results on the Brown corpus.">

The paper that started neural language modeling. It presents itself as a fight against the curse of dimensionality of section 2.3, using a learned vector for each word: *"having seen the sentence 'The cat is walking in the bedroom' in the training corpus should help us generalize to make the sentence 'A dog was running in a room' almost as likely"*. On the Brown corpus (about a million words), a neural model gets a test perplexity of 268, against 321 for Kneser-Ney smoothing, and 252 when it is mixed half and half with a trigram model. But it was slow: 5 epochs on the 14 million words of AP News took 3 weeks on 40 processors, mostly to compute the softmax over the whole vocabulary.

</PaperNote>

$$
q(x_{L+1} = v \mid x_1 \dots x_L) = \text{softmax}\big(U \tanh(A c + b) + d\big)_v,
$$

where $A$ and $b$ are the weights and the biases of the hidden layer, and $U$ and $d$ those of the output layer. The output layer gives one score $z_v$ per word $v$ of the vocabulary, and the softmax, $\text{softmax}(z)_v = e^{z_v} / \sum_{w} e^{z_w}$, turns these V scores into probabilities, positive and summing to 1, as the sigmoid of section 3.2 did for two classes.

The embedding matrix $W$ is a parameter of the model, like $A$, $b$, $U$ and $d$: **the embeddings and the weights of the network are learned jointly**, by minimizing the cross-entropy loss of section 2.2 on the training text. At each step, the gradient of the loss flows back through the network to the columns of $W$ of the words of the context, and moves them.

I'll assume here that you know how to train a neural network with PyTorch. If not, [the course of Andrej Karpathy](https://karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero.html) is a great place to start, and its third lecture builds a model of this kind, on characters.

<Optional title="PyTorch" subtitle="what we use from it in this post">

From here on, the exercises train neural networks with PyTorch. They use only a small part of it, and mostly the same part:
- **Tensors.** `torch.tensor` turns lists of word indices into tensors. `.to(device)` moves them to the GPU when there is one, and the code runs on the CPU otherwise.
- **Layers.** A model is a stack of layers in `nn.Sequential`: `nn.Embedding`, which holds the embedding matrix of section 4.1; `nn.Flatten`, which concatenates the embeddings of the context; `nn.Linear`; and the activations `nn.Tanh` and `nn.ReLU`.
- **Losses.** `F.cross_entropy` takes the V scores of the output layer and the index of the right word, applies the softmax, and computes the cross-entropy of section 2.2. `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` does the same for two classes, with the sigmoid of section 3.2.

The training loop is written by hand, and it is the same in every exercise:

```python
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    order = torch.randperm(len(targets))  # shuffle the examples
    for start in range(0, len(order), batch_size):
        batch = order[start:start + batch_size]
        scores = model(inputs[batch])
        loss = F.cross_entropy(scores, targets[batch])
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()   # compute the gradients
        optimizer.step()  # update the parameters
```

To measure a model on the validation set, put the computation inside `with torch.no_grad():`, which skips the gradients. To keep the model of the best epoch, save a copy of it with `copy.deepcopy`. We don't use `Dataset` or `DataLoader`: the data of each exercise fits in memory as a few tensors, and we draw the batches ourselves.

Later sections add a few pieces: `requires_grad_(False)`, to keep pre-trained embeddings fixed during the training (section 5); the recurrent layers `nn.LSTM` and `nn.GRU`, and gradient clipping with `nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_` (section 7); and models written as a subclass of `nn.Module`, with their own `forward` method, when they are not a simple stack of layers, like the LSTM of section 7 and the encoder–decoder of section 9.

</Optional>

Why would this work better than counts? Remember the curse of dimensionality of section 2.3: most contexts of 3 or 4 words never appear in the training text, and for the counts, an unseen context is a dead end. The neural model is not stuck: if it has seen "the cat is walking", and if "dog" has an embedding close to the one of "cat", then it already knows something about "the dog is walking". And similar words do get similar embeddings, because they appear in similar contexts and must lead to similar predictions. **The model generalizes from the contexts it has seen to the contexts it has never seen.**

### 4.3 Ways to aggregate the word vectors of a text

Concatenation works for language modeling, because we can assume that only the last n − 1 words matter. But as we said in section 3.1, it does not work at all for classification: the sentiment of a review can be anywhere in it. How can we aggregate the L embeddings of a review?

The simplest option is to average them. And there is a neat way to write this average. Adding up the embeddings of the words of a review is the same as multiplying $W$ by the bag-of-words vector $C$ of section 3.2, since the column of each word is added as many times as the word appears. So

$$
\frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} e_i = \frac{1}{L} \, W C = W f,
$$

where $f = C / L$ is the vector of the *frequencies* of the words in the review. **The average of the embeddings is a bag of words, compressed by $W$ from V to D dimensions.** Like the bag of words, it forgets the order of the words. And it gives the same importance to all the words, "the" included: to avoid diluting the average with uninformative words, we can weight it with the IDF of section 3.3.

What do we gain with this compression? Consider the simplest head, a logistic regression on the average: $p(\text{positive}) = \sigma(b + h \cdot W f)$, where $h$ is the vector of the weights of the head. Expanding the dot product gives

$$
h \cdot W f = \sum_{v=1}^{V} (h \cdot w_v) \, f_v,
$$

where $w_v$ is the column of $W$ for the word $v$. This is a logistic regression on the frequencies of the words, where the weight of the word $v$ is $h \cdot w_v$. So if $W$ is learned along with $h$, nothing is gained over section 3! The gains can come from two places:
- add hidden layers after the average, so that the head is not linear anymore;
- start from an embedding matrix $W$ that is already good, and keep it fixed. Then the weight $h \cdot w_v$ of a word depends on its embedding, so similar words get similar weights, even the words that never appear in the labeled reviews. This is the topic of section 5.

The first option is the *deep averaging network* (DAN) [@iyyer2015]: average the embeddings, pass the average through a few hidden layers, then classify. In 2015, it rivaled the models that followed the syntax of the sentences, while training in a few minutes on a laptop. Let's train one on IMDB.

### 4.4 Embeddings are embeddings

We have now learned two embedding matrices, for two tasks. Yet the words are the same in both: "superb" means the same in a Wikipedia article and in a movie review, so we could expect its embedding to be similar whatever the task. Let's compare the two matrices.

<WhatToNotice>

The two matrices are the mirror image of each other. The averaging network only learned what the sentiment needs: whether a word is positive or negative. It knows that "excellent" is like "wonderfully" and "terrible" like "awful", which the language model did not learn. But "three", "he", "city" and "film" say nothing about the sentiment, so the training had no consistent reason to move their embeddings, and their neighbors look random. The language model did the opposite: it organized the frequent words of Wikipedia, and left the rare words of praise and blame in the noise. **An embedding learned for a task only captures what this task needs, from the data of this task.**

</WhatToNotice>

</Exercise>

This is the limit of learning the embeddings along with the task. A labeled dataset is small, and it only teaches what the task needs: 20,000 reviews are enough to learn which words are positive, but not that "film" is like "book". Yet the meanings of the words do not depend on the task. Learning embeddings that reflect the structure of the language again for every task, from a few labeled examples, looks like a waste.

**What if we could learn the embeddings once and for all, on a task where data is abundant, and then reuse them for many tasks?** Exercise 4.2 gives a hint of where to find such a task: predicting a word from its context only needs raw text, which is free, and it taught our language model that "three" is like "four" and "film" like "book". This is the idea of *pre-training* and *transfer learning*, and the topic of the next section.
