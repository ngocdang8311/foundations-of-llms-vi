## 1. The typical tasks of NLP

Now that we live in a world of LLMs, it's easy to think that autoregressive language modeling is all there is to NLP. But before 2019 and the realization that GPT-2 could generate coherent text and hence do other tasks without being specifically trained for them, language modeling was only one among the many tasks of classical NLP.

For decades, **text classification, tagging and translation** were the most important challenges that were tackled using the tools of machine learning.

Each of these four tasks can be formally represented as **learning certain probability distributions conditioned on an input piece of text** seen as a sequence of words. This is illustrated below.

Let's consider these different tasks.

### 1.1 Classification

This is the simplest task to state in NLP: you receive a piece of text and you have to classify it among a predefined list of categories. It can be spam detection, sentiment analysis (*Is this movie review positive or not?*), or classification among topics (*Is this news article about sports, politics, technology...?*).

For that, a classifier has to learn the conditional probability

$$
p(C \mid x_1 \dots x_L)
$$

that a certain sequence of words $x_1 \dots x_L$ belongs to a class $C$, where here **$L$ is not fixed a priori, and depends on the sequence.**

As shown in the introductory example at the top of this post (*"Wow, what a masterpiece. [...] A perfect film if you love naps."*), sentiment classification can be subtle and involve detecting things like sarcasm.

### 1.2 Language modeling

In principle, modeling a language means learning the probability distribution $p(x_1 \dots x_L)$ of its possible sequences of words. In practice, it is often more convenient to learn the *next-word probability*

$$
p(x_{L+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_L).
$$

From this next-word probability, we can simply recover the full probability by using the chain rule, for instance

$$
p(x_1 x_2 x_3 x_4) = p(x_1)\, p(x_2 \mid x_1)\, p(x_3 \mid x_1 x_2)\, p(x_4 \mid x_1 x_2 x_3)
$$

From today's perspective, the main use of such a probability distribution is to generate text using autoregression: *draw a word from the next-word distribution, append it to the text, and repeat*. This is the principle behind LLM chatbots and agents.

But knowing the language probability distribution can have other uses, for instance to score the probability of sentences among a list of candidates. Imagine an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model producing two candidate transcriptions that both reasonably fit the audio sample: it can be followed by a post-processing stage using a language model, to score these candidates and help choose the right one. For instance on any reasonable language model, the probability of the sentence *"General relativity was formulated by **Alfred** Einstein"* would certainly rank lower than that of *"General relativity was formulated by **Albert** Einstein"*.

Although I present them separately, it is important to realize that **next-word prediction is a form of classification task!** Instead of assigning probabilities to a finite set of classes, you assign probabilities to a finite set of words among the pre-defined vocabulary. The main practical difference is the size: generally a few classes in NLP classification tasks, while the vocabulary size in language modeling is often tens of thousands.

### 1.3 Translation

Translation from one language to another has long been one of the most important tasks in NLP, but also a hard one.
Formally, we want to learn the probability of a candidate translation $y_1 \dots y_K$ conditioned on the original sentence $x_1 \dots x_L$. Again, in practice, we seek to estimate the next-word probability of the translated sequence, conditioned on the original sentence and what has been written so far:

$$
p(y_{i+1} \mid y_1 \dots y_i; x_1 \dots x_L)
$$

Note that learning this probability distribution is far from obvious since the two sentences a priori have different lengths, and the translation is generally not a word-for-word correspondence.

### 1.4 Tagging

Finally, another typical NLP task is *tagging*. Its goal is to assign a class per word (or group of words). It can be grammar mistake detection, or tagging the grammatical category of each word in a sentence (noun, verb, ...), a task that is known as *part-of-speech* tagging.

It can also be *Named Entity Recognition* (NER), for instance tagging all the names of people (or all places) in a text.

Tagging can be seen as "a classifier applied to each word", but each taking into account the surrounding context.

$$
p(t_i \mid x_1 \dots x_L)
$$

I will not cover much of tagging here.
