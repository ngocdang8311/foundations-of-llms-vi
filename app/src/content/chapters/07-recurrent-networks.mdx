## 7. Recurrent networks: reading in order

At the end of section 6, we saw that **none of our models reads a text in order**. The n-gram models and Bengio's model read the last few words in order, and forget everything before them. The bags of words and the averaging networks read all the words, but forget their order. Consider again these two hard reviews:

*A: I expected it to be terrible, but it was great.*

*B: I expected it to be great, but it was terrible.*

They have exactly the same words, so every classifier of the post so far gives them exactly the same probability, and gets one of them wrong. To tell them apart, the encoder must aggregate the embeddings of the words in a way that depends on their order. Before transformers, there were two main ways to do this.

The first one is the **convolution**. Take the network of Bengio's model (section 4.2), which reads the concatenated embeddings of a window of a few consecutive words. Instead of applying it only to the last words of the text, slide it along the text, and apply it to every window. Each window gives a small vector of *features*, and one of these features could for instance react to "was terrible", wherever it appears in the review. Then keep, for each feature, its largest value over all the windows of the text. This is called *max pooling*, and it gives a vector of fixed size, whatever the length of the text. Collobert et al. used it to tag the words of a sentence [@collobert2011], and Kim to classify sentences.

The second one is the **recurrent network**. It reads the words one after the other, as we do, and updates a vector, its *state*, after each word. The state after the last word is the representation of the text.

A convolution sees the order of the words inside each window, but not beyond. With windows of 3 words, "was terrible" and "was great" give different features, which is enough for our two reviews. But a window cannot relate two words that are farther apart than its size, unless several layers of convolutions are stacked. A recurrent network has no window: in principle, its last state depends on all the words of the text, and on their order. In the years before transformers, recurrent networks were the standard encoder of NLP, and they are the topic of this section.

### 7.1 The RNN

A *recurrent neural network* (RNN) reads the embeddings $x_1 \dots x_L$ of the words of a text one at a time. It keeps a *hidden state* $h_t$, a vector of size H, and updates it after each word:

$$
h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b),
$$

starting from $h_0 = 0$. The matrices $W_h$, of size H × H, and $W_x$, of size H × D, and the bias $b$ are the same at every step. This is why an RNN can read texts of any length with a fixed number of parameters, like the convolution, whose window has the same weights at every position.

<PaperNote id="elman1990" look="Figure 7: the tree of the 29 words of the lexicon, grouped by their average hidden state. The network was never told which words are nouns or verbs.">

Elman's "simple recurrent network" is the RNN of this section, with a sigmoid instead of the tanh: after each word, the hidden units are copied into "context units", which are read with the next word. He trained it to predict the next word of 10,000 short sentences like "man eat cookie" or "dragon chase cat", made from 29 words and a few templates. Each word was a one-hot vector, so the network knew nothing about them. Yet when Elman grouped the words by their average hidden state, the tree separated the verbs from the nouns, the animate nouns from the inanimate ones, the humans from the animals, and the food from the breakable objects. Jay McClelland suggested a metaphor for the task, which Elman calls "humorous—but entirely accurate": "It is like trying to learn a language by listening to the radio."

</PaperNote>

The state $h_t$ depends on all the words $x_1 \dots x_t$, and on their order: reading "terrible" then "great" does not give the same state as reading "great" then "terrible", since each word is added to a state that has already been transformed by $W_h$ and the tanh. So the state is a summary of everything read so far, of fixed size H, updated at each word. For classification, **the last state $h_L$ is the fixed-size representation of the text**, and a head reads it, for instance a logistic regression as in section 3.2:

$$
p(\text{positive} \mid x_1 \dots x_L) = \sigma(w \cdot h_L + c).
$$

The RNN also gives a vector for each word, the state $h_t$ after this word, which is what tagging needs (section 1.4): a classifier applied to each $h_t$ gives a label to each word. But $h_t$ only knows the words up to $t$. For tagging, a second RNN often reads the text backwards, and the two states of each word are concatenated: this is a *bidirectional* RNN. Keep this sequence of states $h_1 \dots h_L$ in mind: in section 9.2, attention will read all of them, instead of the last one only.

### 7.2 The training problem

An RNN is trained like the other networks of the post, by minimizing a loss with gradient descent. To compute the gradient, *unroll* the network: write its L steps one after the other, as in the figure at the start of this section. The unrolled RNN is a deep feedforward network with one layer per word, where all the layers share the same weights, and the gradient is computed by backpropagation through these L layers. This is called *backpropagation through time* [@werbos1990].

This is where the problem lies. For the loss to teach the network something about the first words of a review, the gradient must travel back from the last state to the first ones, through all the steps in between. By the chain rule, the gradient of the last state with respect to the state after word $t$ is a product of $L - t$ matrices, one per step:

$$
\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial h_t} = \frac{\partial h_L}{\partial h_{L-1}} \cdots \frac{\partial h_{t+1}}{\partial h_t}, \qquad \text{with} \quad \frac{\partial h_s}{\partial h_{s-1}} = \text{diag}\big(1 - h_s^2\big)\, W_h,
$$

where the diagonal matrix holds the derivative of the tanh, $1 - \tanh^2$, for each component of the state. Like the powers of a number, such a product tends to shrink exponentially with the distance $L - t$ if its matrices shrink the vectors they multiply, and to grow exponentially if they stretch them. These are the *vanishing* and the *exploding gradients*. The derivative of the tanh is at most 1, and close to 0 when a unit is saturated, so the gradient tends to vanish. Then the first words of a long review receive almost no gradient, and the network cannot learn to use them, even though they are in its input.

Bengio, Simard and Frasconi showed that this is not bad luck [@bengio1994]. A network that stores a piece of information robustly, so that the noise of the following inputs does not erase it, is precisely a network whose gradients with respect to the past vanish exponentially. In their words: "These results expose a trade-off between efficient learning by gradient descent and latching on information for long periods."

<PaperNote id="hochreiter1991" look="Section 2.3, page 21 (in German): one paragraph that states the exponential dependence, and its consequences for the learning rate and the initialization.">

Sepp Hochreiter wrote this diploma thesis in Munich, advised by Jürgen Schmidhuber. It contains the first analysis of the problem: the error sent back u steps in time is multiplied by a product of u factors, each a derivative times a weight, so it shrinks or grows exponentially with u. Setting this product close to 1, he writes, "is very difficult and slow, because it needs a very small learning rate" (our translation). He had also seen the explosion in practice: weights that grew until the floating-point numbers overflowed. And the thesis already proposes the remedy of the LSTM: a unit connected to itself with a fixed weight, through which the error flows back unchanged.

</PaperNote>

When the gradient explodes, a single update can throw the weights far away, and the loss suddenly jumps. The fix is simple, and it is called *gradient clipping* [@pascanu2013]: before each update, if the norm of the gradient is larger than a threshold, divide the gradient so that its norm equals the threshold. The update keeps its direction, but its size is limited. In PyTorch, it is one line, `nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)`, between `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()`.

Vanishing gradients need a change of architecture. The **LSTM** (for *long short-term memory*) adds a second state, the *cell* $c_t$, which is updated by an addition instead of a product by a matrix:

$$
c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t),
$$

where $\odot$ is the product component by component. The vector $g_t = \tanh(W_g [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_g)$ is a candidate update, computed from the previous state and the current word, like the state of the plain RNN. And $f_t$, $i_t$ and $o_t$ are three *gates*: vectors of numbers between 0 and 1, computed in the same way but with a sigmoid, for instance $f_t = \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)$, each with its own weights. The *forget gate* $f_t$ says how much of each component of the cell is kept, the *input gate* $i_t$ how much of the candidate is added, and the *output gate* $o_t$ how much of the cell goes into the state $h_t$, which is what the next step and the head read.

From $c_{t-1}$ to $c_t$, the gradient is only multiplied by the forget gate, component by component: no matrix, and no derivative of the tanh. **When the forget gate is close to 1, the gradient goes back through many steps almost unchanged**, and the network learns, word by word, what to keep and what to forget.

The *GRU* (for *gated recurrent unit*) [@cho2014a] is a simpler variant, with two gates and no separate cell. In PyTorch, `nn.RNN`, `nn.LSTM` and `nn.GRU` take the same arguments, so you can switch from one to the other in one line. Let's measure the gradient that reaches each word in these networks.

<WhatToNotice>

On a log scale, the curves of the RNN and of the LSTM are almost straight lines: at each step back, the gradient is divided by about 2.2 for the RNN, and by about 1.6 for the LSTM. After 99 steps, the first word gets almost nothing. So the LSTM is not a magic fix. With the default initialization of PyTorch, the biases of its forget gate are close to 0, so the gate starts around $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, and, as Jozefowicz et al. put it, "this introduces a vanishing gradient with a factor of 0.5 per timestep" [@jozefowicz2015]. With a bias of 3, the forget gate starts around $\sigma(3) \approx 0.95$, and the gradient stays between 0.25 and 0.5 from the 10th word back to the first one. This is what the LSTM adds: a path where the gradient is only multiplied by the forget gates, which the network can learn to open. The plain RNN has no such path: at every step, its gradient goes through $W_h$ and the derivative of the tanh. Jozefowicz et al. recommend "adding a bias of 1 to the forget gate of every LSTM in every application". **In an LSTM, the forget gates decide how far back the gradient goes.**

</WhatToNotice>

</Exercise>

<PaperNote id="hochreiter1997" look="Table 2: BPTT and RTRL (another way to compute the gradient of an RNN) against the LSTM, on the same task. With a delay of 100 steps, BPTT and RTRL never succeed.">

The LSTM of 1997 had no forget gate. Its cell was updated by $c_t = c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t$: a self-connection with a fixed weight of 1, which the authors call the "constant error carousel", since the error goes around it without changing. The two gates "learn to open and close access to the constant error flow". On artificial tasks designed to need a long memory, the LSTM bridged "minimal time lags in excess of 1000 discrete-time steps". With a delay of 100 steps, BPTT and RTRL did not succeed once in 5 million training sequences, while the LSTM succeeded in every run, after about 5,000 sequences. The forget gate was added in 2000 by Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins: on long streams of inputs that were not cut into sequences, the cell grew without limit, and the network had to learn to reset it.

</PaperNote>

### 7.3 RNNs as language models and generators

Let's go back to language modeling. An RNN is also a language model: at each position $t$, a head reads the state $h_t$ and gives the probabilities of the next word, with a softmax over the vocabulary, as in Bengio's model:

$$
q(x_{t+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_t) = \text{softmax}(U h_t + d).
$$

The state $h_t$ depends on all the words since the start of the text, so **the context is no longer limited to the last n − 1 words**. Mikolov et al. built such a model in 2010 [@mikolov2010], and with a mix of several RNNs, they reduced the perplexity of a Kneser-Ney 5-gram model on news articles by almost half. In their words, the results were "breaking myth that language modeling is just about counting n-grams".

To train it, we would like to unroll the RNN over the whole training text and backpropagate through it. But WikiText-2 is a single text of 2 million words, and the gradient would have to go through 2 million steps. The usual solution is *truncated backpropagation through time*. Cut the text into chunks of 35 words, and read them in order. The state is carried from one chunk to the next, so that the prediction of each word still depends on all the text before it. But the gradient stops at the start of the chunk: the network learns from at most 35 words of context, even if it uses more. To use the parallelism of matrix products, we also cut the text into 32 long streams, and a batch reads the 32 streams side by side, one chunk of each.

Like Bengio's model in exercise 4.2, this model has many parameters (most of them in the output layer, one row of $U$ per word of the vocabulary) and it overfits after a few epochs, so we keep the model of the epoch with the best validation perplexity.

Our LSTM is small, and it was trained for only 3 epochs. The best LSTMs of 2016 on WikiText-2 have two layers of 650 units, and they are regularized with *dropout*: during the training, components of the vectors of the network are set to zero at random, so that the network cannot rely too much on any of them. Zaremba et al. applied dropout to the embeddings and to the states that go from one layer to the next, but not to the state that goes from one word to the next: new random zeros at every word would also erase what the network remembers [@zaremba2014]. Gal and Ghahramani then showed that this state can be dropped too, if the same components are set to zero at every word of a sequence [@gal2016]. With this *variational dropout*, a two-layer LSTM reaches a test perplexity of 96.3 on WikiText-2, and 80.8 with a mechanism that can also copy a word from the recent text [@merity2016]. We will meet an even better LSTM in section 8.

Like the n-gram models, an RNN language model can generate text: draw the next word from its probabilities, feed it back as the next input, and repeat. RNNs made this popular at the level of characters. Instead of words, the model reads and predicts one character at a time, with a vocabulary of a few dozen symbols. In a famous blog post, Andrej Karpathy trained such LSTMs on the works of Shakespeare, on Wikipedia, on LaTeX files and on the source code of Linux [@karpathy2015]. As he notes about Shakespeare, "all the RNN knows are characters, so in particular it samples both speaker's names and the contents". Graves trains seven layers of LSTM (700 cells each, 21 million weights) to predict the next byte of the first 96 million bytes of the English Wikipedia. The gradient is cut every 100 bytes, as in exercise 7.3, but the state is carried over 100 sequences, so the network can use up to 10,000 characters of context. It reaches 1.67 bits per character on held-out text, against "more than 2" for compressors like zip. In its samples, the network closes the brackets and the XML tags it opens, and it has learned "a subword model that enables it to invent feasible-looking words and names", like "Lochroom River" or "swalloped". The second half of the paper does the same with handwriting: the network writes a text one pen stroke at a time.

At first, the characters are drawn at random. After 100 batches, the model has learned the spaces, the line breaks and a few short words. After 300 batches, it writes in the format of a play: a name in capitals followed by a colon, then the lines of this character, like "GOLUTE:", a name that Shakespeare never wrote. After 1,000 batches, the names are real ones, like JULIET or FRIAR LAURENCE, and most words are English words, in sentences that make no sense.

<Note title="Words, characters or subwords?" emoji="🔤" variant="info">

So far, our units have been words. A word-level model has a fixed vocabulary: in WikiText-2, the words that appear at least 3 times in the training text, every other word being replaced by `<unk>`. A word outside the vocabulary, like "Zendaya" or "flabbergasting" in section 3, is invisible, or at best an `<unk>`, which says nothing about it. And each word gets its own embedding, learned separately, so the model must learn "film" and "films" twice.

Characters solve this problem: TinyShakespeare has only 65 different characters, and any word can be written with them, even a word that the model has never seen. But a text has about 5 times more characters than words, so the RNN must carry information over 5 times more steps, which is exactly what section 7.2 says is hard. And the model must learn to spell before it can learn anything about meaning.

Note that perplexities measured on different units cannot be compared directly. The probability that a model gives to a whole text does not depend on the unit, but the perplexity divides its logarithm by the number of units: words, characters, or anything else. For instance, the character-level LSTM of the figure above ends with 2.0 bits per character on the validation text of TinyShakespeare: a perplexity of $2^{2.0} = 4$ per character, which looks much better than the perplexities of our word-level models. But this text has 5.5 characters per word, spaces and punctuation included, so the same probability is $2.0 \times 5.5 \approx 11$ bits per word: a perplexity of about $2^{11} \approx 2{,}000$ per word. To compare a character-level model with a word-level model, divide the log-probability of the same text by the same number, for instance its number of words.

*Subwords* are the compromise used by today's language models: frequent words are units of their own, and rare words are cut into frequent pieces. Byte-pair encoding [@sennrich2016] builds such a vocabulary from the training text: start from the characters, and merge the most frequent pair of consecutive symbols into a new symbol, again and again, until the vocabulary has the size you want. With subwords, no word is unknown, and the texts are only a little longer than with words. These are the tokenizers, the topic of the next post.

</Note>

<PaperNote id="bojanowski2017" look="Table 6: the character n-grams that weigh the most in a few words. They are often morphemes: “fahr” and “fahrer” in the German autofahrer, “ness>” in kindness.">

By the team of fastText (section 4.3), this paper solves the problem of unknown words for embeddings. Each word is represented by its character n-grams, for n from 3 to 6, with `<` and `>` to mark its start and its end: with n = 3, "where" gives `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere` and `re>`, plus the whole word, `<where>`. The vector of a word is the sum of the vectors of its n-grams, trained with the skip-gram model of section 5.2. So "Zendaya" and "flabbergasting" get a vector even if they never appear in the training text, built from pieces that do. The gain is largest for rare words and for languages with many forms per word: on a German test of word similarity, the model trained on 5% of the text does better than the CBOW of word2vec trained on all of it.

</PaperNote>
